# Reading the Series

**Status:** Draft
**Purpose:** Companion document for the meta-deck in the SSDLC in the Mythos Era series. Provides a guide to which deck answers which question, intended to support both linear first reads and on-the-fly navigation during discussion.

---

## Why This Deck Exists

The series is eight artifacts. A reader new to it does not need to read all eight before engaging with the argument, and a reader returning to it for a specific question should not have to re-derive the structure to find the right deck. This deck names what each deck does and when to open it.

The deck is organized by deck number rather than by reader journey. That sequencing makes the meta-deck usable as a starting point with audiences who are already partially familiar — flip to the relevant deck during discussion, skip the ones the audience already owns. A first-time reader can read top-to-bottom; the order matches the dependency structure of the series.

The closing slide reframes the work. The rest of the series is unflinching about the change in the threat landscape. This deck closes by naming what the discipline produces — not absence of threat, but a compounding advantage relative to organizations that defer the work.

## The Decks

### Thesis. The rigor floor has risen.

Motivates the rest of the series. Argues that attacker attention used to be a scarce resource that quietly subsidized the SSDLC of every organization that was not safety-critical, and that frontier model capability has removed that subsidy. The argument is multi-source — it does not depend on any one vendor's claims.

When to open: first read; any time the threat picture is questioned; any time a stakeholder needs to understand why the change is structural rather than incidental.

### Deck 1. Classification.

Names the two axes — exposure and project shape — that precede every control decision. Exposure drives which controls are mandatory and how aggressive they need to be. Project shape drives how those controls are implemented and sequenced. Collapsing the two produces wrong answers in both directions.

When to open: starting a new project; auditing an existing one against the new floor; when control decisions are being made without an explicit classification step underneath them.

### Deck 2. Placement.

Names where each control runs along the stage-cost gradient. Every control has two costs — elapsed time and resource — and both budgets expand as a change moves through the SSDLC. Controls fit a stage when both costs fit both budgets. Controls that fit multiple stages should run at the earliest one whose budgets they fit.

When to open: deciding what runs at PR cadence versus release-candidate cadence; when someone proposes putting an expensive control too early in the pipeline; when AI-driven analysis needs to be placed against its real cost profile rather than vendor narrative.

### Deck 3. Tool Properties.

Specifies what a tool must do to fit the architecture before naming any specific tool. The upstream specification that procurement decisions answer to. Without it, tool selection becomes vendor narrative.

When to open: before any procurement conversation; when a tool decision is being argued without a specification underneath it; when the question "is this the right tool" cannot be answered because the criteria were never written down.

### Deck 4. Tool Selection.

Picks specific tools against the upstream specification. The most replaceable decision in the sequence — by design. Specific tools turn over; the specification they answered to should not.

When to open: actually selecting vendors; revisiting a tool choice when the tool changes or the requirements drift; when an audience wants to debate vendors before the upstream work is done.

### Deck 5. AI Roles.

Names where AI fits across the architecture beyond the late-stage analysis role most narratives assume. Three placements, three accountability boundaries. Corrects the conclusion that AI is a single thing with a single home in the pipeline.

When to open: justifying a specific AI integration choice; pushing back on AI overreach; when AI's role in the architecture has to be defended against either over-optimism or over-skepticism.

### Deck 6. Economics.

Names the cost structure the new environment imposes. Generation cost is going down; response cost is going up; the two are independent forces on the same P&L. Engineering's mandate is to capture the downward driver. Security's mandate is to operate efficiently under the upward driver. Treating the two as opposed is the failure mode.

When to open: the budget conversation with finance; defending the security function's investment against the implicit frame of "hold the line at previous spend"; when leadership is treating engineering's AI adoption and security's response cost as a single tradeoff rather than two independent forces.

### Deck 7. Agentic Oversight.

Names the oversight role for agentic AI in the architecture and the handoff to human decision-making that role requires. The agent watches everything, identifies miscalibration faster than human review cadence allows, and produces a traceable artifact that humans use to make auditable decisions. Traceability is the bar, not reproducibility.

When to open: deciding what an agent is allowed to do; defending the traceability-not-reproducibility standard against an audit team that expects reproducibility; when "AI everywhere" is being argued as a strategy and the response needs to be "agents for the irreducibly ad hoc work, fixed processes for everything else."

## How to Start

Tooling can be procured in weeks. Organizational discipline takes quarters. Frontier capability is not waiting — Mythos is one model, and the next one will deepen the gap. Every quarter of delay is a quarter operating against an advancing floor without the discipline that makes the floor manageable. The catchup distance does not stay fixed.

Organizations that start the discipline now keep pace with the capability curve. The ones that wait keep falling behind. The asymmetry compounds, and it favors whoever starts earlier — not because they avoid the threat but because the rate at which they accumulate discipline catches up with the rate at which capability advances.

The compelling event is not Mythos. The compelling event is the gap between two clocks — capability advancing, discipline accumulating — that widens until someone closes it. Closing it is the work the series describes.
